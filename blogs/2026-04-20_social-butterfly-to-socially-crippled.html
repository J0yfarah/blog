From Social Butterfly
to Socially Awkward

Date
April 20, 2026

Read
5 min

Author
Farah Tlemcani

Before coming to the Netherlands, I was so excited. New life, new location, new opportunities — this tiny country, extremely opposite to everything I knew, that supposedly holds infinite possibilities. I would thrive! I said to myself. I would literally shine. I was already daydreaming about my next big-girl tech job and an even bigger social circle.

But little did I know. And oh boy, had I been warned enough.

It feels invisible until it's gone. You either deal with it, or you live with it.

Who I Used to Be

To give you some context: I am — or at least used to be — a social butterfly. I participated in networking events, was an active member of uni clubs, hosted tech workshops (and crafts workshops, but that's a story for another day 😄), managed teams, did hackathons, and actually won a couple. I was smart, yes. Resourceful, indeed. But my social skills were my biggest asset. I thrived in busy, diverse environments where different topics collided and different people showed up.

And then: the big move. Also, the big wedding — because why have one life-altering event when you can have two? I got married and crossed the Mediterranean to the North Sea in the same season. Two massive changes, arriving together, uninvited to the same party that was supposed to be my fresh start.

And then it started hitting.

The Crash

I had never experienced social anxiety the way I did here. Suddenly I was stuttering talking to my neighbour. Avoiding eye contact with strangers on the street. Mixing up words with a recruiter on the phone in a way that made me sound like a total impostor in my own industry. I had lost it — my treasure, my strongest natural skill — and I couldn't figure out why. (I still can't, fully. What I'm sharing here is merely a hypothesis.)

I didn't even notice at first. Not until I spoke — and somewhat overshared — with a friend I already knew here. And then: mind blown. Revelation bestowed upon me. I was socially crippled, and I was only just finding that out. It was like connecting dots mid-conversation, watching the picture form in real time. She listened to me actively stuttering, trying to explain myself, heartbeat rising and all — and she told me she'd felt the same when she arrived.

We tried to come up with theories on why, on God's earth, this sudden personality shift happens to a person.

The Hypothesis

Theory one — the obvious one

I had spent 25 years in the same spot, surrounded by the same people, speaking the same language, operating within the same cultural reflexes and social norms. It was a comfort zone with its own challenges, sure — but it was home. It was familiar.

And then, suddenly: a whole man is living with me (sorry, dear husband), new house, new country, new neighbours, new job market, new everything. My brain was so flabbergasted by the sudden rerouting of the entire course of my 25 years of existence that it simply… crashed.

I didn't know I was going through it because I couldn't feel that I was going through it. I just felt depressed and anxious, all the time, without being able to name it. It took one honest, heartfelt conversation with a fellow expat to connect the dots: I wasn't socially awkward because I was depressed. I was depressed because I had become socially awkward. My life had switched servers and the ping went 999.

→ A note on place

This isn't about the Netherlands specifically. Swap it for Canada, Germany, or any country where you rebuild from zero, and I believe the story would be the same. This country just happened to be the setting. Uprooting your entire life — your language, your people, your reflexes — is the variable. The destination is almost irrelevant.

The Fix (Work in Progress)

Approach one — actually falling in love with where you are

Yes, I know what a tikkie is now. No, I no longer buy stroopwafels from tourist traps. And gewoon is basically my holy grail at this point. 

Just kidding, This is so corny and generic.

To be honest, I genuinely love it here. Not in a "I have to say this" way — in a I-stare-at-my-window-for-twenty-minutes-every-morning way. I live in IJburg, and from my big window I can see a small dock. Boats come and go, set sail, drift back. It's quietly stunning in a way I didn't expect from a country this flat. The infrastructure, the architecture, the water everywhere — it got me.

And the directness? I actually love it. I know it reads as harsh to a lot of people, especially coming from cultures where feedback is wrapped in five layers of softening. But give me honest criticism over polite ambiguity any day. In a work setting, someone telling you plainly what isn't working is a gift — it tells you exactly where to improve, exactly what to build on. I'll take blunt over vague every single time.

What genuinely moved me most though is the diversity living inside a single building. People from all over the world — different languages, different backgrounds, different ideas of what a neighbourhood even means — organizing events, doing community work, actively trying to know each other. That kind of intentional proximity is something I hadn't experienced before, and it matters more to me than I expected. It reminded me that belonging isn't about sameness. It's about showing up.

Approach two — crochet

I know. Bear with me.

I'm a crafty person — I've been crocheting since I was 10, which helped a lot with pattern recognition, no pun intended. So I joined my building's art club, where we sometimes teach beginners and sometimes just sit on cozy couches in the shared living space, tangled in yarn and gossip. Young grannies, honestly. Shoutout to Van der Linden for the amazing shared spaces — 10/10, would recommend.

Through those couches I met Dutch, German, Danish, North African people — different backgrounds, all sitting together, yarn in hand. And it works. Technically still working — it's a long process — but I felt like myself again. It gave me my energy back in small, manageable doses.

Closing Thoughts

Every expat blog covers the external Netherlands — the housing, the bureaucracy, the bikes, the weather. But not many talk about what starting completely over quietly does to you internally, especially if you come from a more communal culture where your social confidence was never something you thought to protect, because it had never been threatened.

If you moved somewhere new and you feel like a dimmer version of yourself: you're not imagining it. You're not broken. You just switched servers. And the ping does, eventually, come back down.
