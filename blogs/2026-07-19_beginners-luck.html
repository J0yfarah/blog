Beginner's Luck:
Notes from a Crochet Workshop in Eindhoven

Date
July 19, 2026

Read
9 min

Author
Farah Tlemcani

Not everything this year has been about job hunting, even though it's felt that way lately. This weekend I spent four straight hours teaching a room full of strangers how to crochet a bag, at Al Warsha Studio in Eindhoven. It was my first ever workshop as the person running it, not attending it, and I think it did more for my sense of actually belonging here than most of what I've written about on this blog so far.

The Quiet Practice

If you read my post from a few months ago, From Social Butterfly to Socially Awkward, you already know the shape of this. The move here cost me something I didn't know I could lose: the easy, unthinking version of myself who could talk to anyone, anywhere, without rehearsing it first.

Crochet was the quiet fix, back then. It still is. I've been meeting a small group of neighbours here in IJburg every two weeks since, cozy, low-effort, nobody performing anything. We just sit with yarn in our hands and talk. Somewhere in the middle of hooking the same stitch over and over, the overthinking loosens its grip a little. It's hard to spiral about a recruiter's silence when someone next to you is asking whether you want tea.

Slowly, without me really clocking it happening in real time, those sessions gave me something back. Not the old version of myself exactly, but something close, real friendships, less awkwardness, fewer of the stuttering, avoid-eye-contact moments I wrote about before.

Everyone Kept Saying the Same Thing

Somewhere along the way, the feedback started repeating itself. "You should host workshops." "You should sell this, you're actually really good." I heard some version of that enough times that I stopped being able to write it off as politeness.

I mentioned it, half-joking, to my friend Amina, who owns Al Warsha Studio and is one of the most genuinely talented people I know. She didn't laugh it off the way I expected. She pushed me, properly, to actually go do it.

What Al Warsha Gave Me

→ A note on starting small

Hosting your first ever workshop is a lot of moving parts for one person: sourcing materials, managing bookings, actually running the room for four hours straight. Al Warsha handled the "suivi" for me, the follow-through, the sourcing, the bookings, even helping animate the session itself, so that I could just focus on teaching. That kind of support is the entire reason a small, first-time creator gets to have a first workshop at all instead of quietly shelving the idea.

I don't think I'd have actually done this without that push and that support. There's a version of this year where "you should host a workshop" just stays a nice thing people said to me once. Instead, I got to stand in a room and actually do it.

The Design

I designed the bag before anything else, on purpose. Two simple stitches, nothing fancy, the actual basics. The goal was that everyone walks out four hours later holding something real, a full, stylish, wearable bag, not just "I learned how to chain 80 and make a foundation row." Concrete beats impressive, especially on day one.

The room itself was the best part before we'd even picked up a hook. Dutch, Indian, Syrian, Egyptian, Palestinian, all in one room, all leaning over the same two stitches at the same time.

As If We'd Known Each Other for Years

At some point the actual teaching quietly turned into just sitting together. Hands busy, yarn everywhere, gossiping about life and work like we'd known each other for years instead of hours. That's the part nobody puts in the workshop description, and it might be the actual product. The bag is just what you get to take home.

The Accidental Lesson

I told everyone upfront: don't follow my pattern blindly. If you mess up a stitch, that's not a mistake to panic over, that might just be a new design decision. Keep going and see what it turns into.

They took that far more seriously than I expected. By the end we had a full spread of colors and shapes and little personal touches nobody else in the room had, and if I'm honest, a lot of them made mine look boring by comparison.

That actually taught me something I didn't walk in expecting to learn. Being a beginner is a real advantage. Once you know the rules, once a process is fully internalized, you follow it automatically, and what you make is structured and clean and beautiful, and also reproducible, the same shape everyone who knows the same rules would make. Beginners don't have that constraint yet. They experiment because they don't know they're not supposed to, and what comes out the other side is stranger and more personal and, more often than not, better.

Closing the Loop

Back in April, I wrote that starting over here had switched something off in me, that the ping had gone to 999 and I didn't know when it would come back down. I didn't have an ending for that post because I didn't have one yet.

This is the closest thing to an update I have: it's coming down. Slowly, in the unglamorous form of biweekly yarn sessions with neighbours and a four-hour Saturday teaching strangers how to make a bag. Nothing about any of this looked like healing while it was happening, it just looked like a hobby. But somewhere inside the low-effort, cozy version of showing up every two weeks, I found actual life lessons, about starting small, about letting people push you, about how being a beginner again isn't a step backward.

What's Next

I'd love to do this again somewhere new. Amsterdam, maybe? Who knows :3
