Too Much, Not Enough:
Field Notes from the Dutch Tech Job Market

Date
April 20, 2026

Read
6 min

Author
Farah Tlemcani

I graduated in 2024 with a Master's in Parallel and Embedded Industrial Computing. By most definitions, I was prepared. I had two startup experiences under my belt, had led teams, managed stakeholders, built data pipelines, shipped AI models to production, and won a multinational competition doing it. I opened LinkedIn with reasonable confidence.

The market had other plans.

The rejection emails all read the same. "We regret to inform you that we have decided to continue with other candidates." Signed, Workday.

The Shape of the Problem

The Dutch tech job market has a shape. And right now, that shape is a T.

T-shaped professionals: deep expertise in one domain, broad awareness of others, are what companies here are hiring for. What I brought was something closer to a pentagon: data engineering, AI development, business analysis, product ownership, stakeholder management, technical documentation. Startup life does that to you. When you are three people trying to do the work of fifteen, you stop asking "is this my job" and start asking "does this need to get done."

That experience taught me more than any curriculum could. But it also made me genuinely difficult to slot into a job description. Too experienced for junior roles, not specialized enough for mid-level ones. A jack of all trades in a market that has very specific trades in mind.

The Blockers, Ranked

1. The language wall

A significant portion of data and analytics roles in the Netherlands, especially in the public sector, healthcare, and local enterprise, list Dutch fluency as a hard requirement. Not preferred. Required. I would read a job description and fit every technical bullet point, and then hit that last line like a wall.

I am not going to pretend this is not frustrating. It is. But I also understand it. Client-facing roles need communication. Regulated industries need documentation in the local language. It is what it is, and the answer to "it is what it is" is Duolingo, gemeente language courses, and a lot of YouTube.

2. The entry-level paradox

Junior roles in this market are not actually junior. The listings say "0-2 years experience" and then require cloud certifications, production pipeline experience, and familiarity with three different orchestration tools. The entry level has quietly become the mid-level, and nobody updated the salary range to match.

Meanwhile, the candidate pool is enormous. Amsterdam attracts international talent from everywhere. For every role I apply to, I am competing with people who have been doing exactly that one thing for three years, in exactly that stack, with Dutch as a bonus. The automated screening does not care about range. It cares about keywords.

3. The CV ceiling

This is the one that got to me the most. I never had trouble selling myself in a room. Put me in front of a hiring manager and I will make the case. But I could not get in the room. The CV goes in, the automated rejection comes out, and somewhere in between there is an ATS system making decisions I never got to contest.

→ A closed door

Internships seemed like a logical workaround, a lower barrier to entry, a chance to prove value in a real setting. Then I learned that Dutch law restricts internships to enrolled students only. That door was legally closed before I even knocked.

What I Did About It

Complaining is data. But data without action is just noise.

Going deeper - certifications

I enrolled in DataCamp's Data Engineering track and Google's Advanced Data Analytics Professional Certificate and started the online dbt course as well. A cloud certification (Azure or AWS) is next on the list. The logic is simple: if the market wants T-shaped, I will pick my vertical and go deep. Data engineering is where I am pointing that investment. The breadth I already have. The depth is what I am actively building.

Building things that solve real problems

The most honest signal of competence is not just a certificate. It is something that works.

So I started building. My current side project, DZ-PROP-TRUTH, was born out of a real frustration: Algerian real estate has no credible, centralized source of truth. No reliable price indices, no busy-area mapping, no transparent data. Prices are word-of-mouth, listings are scattered across WhatsApp groups and informal platforms, and buyers navigate almost entirely on gut feel. I am building a system to change that: scraping, cleaning, modelling, and eventually publishing price trends per wilaya.

Version 0 is still in progress and I will write about it properly once it ships. But it is the kind of problem I could not stop thinking about, which is usually the right reason to build something. The portfolio follows from the work, not the other way around.

Showing up: tech meetups and actual humans

One of the better decisions I made during this period was installing the Meetup app and actually going to things.

Through it I found communities that genuinely changed the texture of this search. Women in Product NL and PyLadies Amsterdam both deserve a proper shoutout. These are communities actively working to empower women and minorities in tech, and they do it well. Walking into a room full of people who are building things, asking hard questions, and rooting for each other is a different kind of resource than any job board.

Beyond the obvious networking benefit, the meetups gave me something subtler: a real preview of Dutch work culture from the inside. How people communicate in professional settings, what they value, how conversations move. That kind of context is genuinely hard to get from a job listing or a LinkedIn profile, and it has made me a sharper, more informed candidate.

Where I Am Now

Still looking. Still learning. Still building. Still here.

The market is competitive, the language barrier is real, and the automated rejection pipeline is soul-crushing in a very specific, lowercase way. But I have also seen what I can build when given the chance, and I know that evidence is solid.

The ping is high. But the connection is still up.
